
The first thing they teach you in a psychology course is that people are not rational actors. We like to think we are. We make lists, weigh options, and tell ourselves we decided logically. But the research says otherwise, and once you know what to look for, you start seeing it everywhere — including in marketing.

## Why habit loops matter more than messaging

BJ Fogg's work on behaviour design and Charles Duhigg's research on habits both point to the same thing: behaviour change does not happen through information alone. People do not change because they learned something new. They change because a cue, a routine, and a reward lined up correctly.

When I started running campaigns for the Dalhousie Science Society, I noticed that the events with the highest attendance were not the ones we promoted most aggressively. They were the ones that fit into existing student routines. A Thursday event after class, with food, promoted through channels students already checked daily.

We had not designed it that way intentionally. But looking back, it worked because it aligned with the habit loop.

## The psychology of social proof

Robert Cialdini's principle of social proof is probably the most consistently underused tool in student marketing. People look to others to determine what is correct behaviour, especially in ambiguous situations.

This is why Instagram accounts that show people having a good time at events tend to convert better than accounts that show beautifully designed posters. The poster tells you what is happening. The photo of a crowd tells you that other people decided it was worth going.

When I took over the Abstract Cafe's Instagram, one of the first things I changed was the content mix. Less designed promotional content, more photos of real people enjoying the space. Engagement went up. The account grew past 1,000 followers without any paid spend. The product had not changed. The social proof had.

## Framing effects in copywriting

Prospect theory, developed by Kahneman and Tversky, shows that people respond more strongly to losses than to equivalent gains. Losing twenty dollars feels worse than gaining twenty dollars feels good.

This has a direct application to how campaigns are written. "Do not miss Orientation Week's opening night" will outperform "Join us for Orientation Week's opening night" in certain contexts because it activates loss aversion.

This is not manipulation. It is meeting people where their brains already are. The job of good marketing is to reduce friction between someone's existing motivation and the action you want them to take.

## What actually changes behaviour

The uncomfortable truth I have landed on after three years of running campaigns, studying psychology, and watching what actually works: most marketing fails not because of bad design or poor targeting, but because it ignores the person.

The best campaigns I have run started with a question: what does this person already want, and how does what we are offering fit into that? Not: how do we convince someone who does not care to care?

Psychology taught me that motivation is rarely the missing ingredient. The missing ingredient is usually the right cue, at the right time, in the right context. Get that right, and the message almost does not matter.
