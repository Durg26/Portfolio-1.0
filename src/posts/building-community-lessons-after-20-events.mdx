
Three years of planning events has taught me that most of what people think makes an event good is wrong. It is not the venue, or the catering, or the production quality. Those things matter at the margins. What actually makes people feel like they belong somewhere is subtler and harder to replicate.

## The first ten minutes

Every event has a first ten minutes problem. Someone walks in alone. They do not know many people. They are looking for a signal that they made the right call by showing up. If they do not find it in the first ten minutes, they will spend the rest of the night looking for an exit.

During Orientation Week at Dalhousie, we spent a lot of time thinking about arrival. What does a person see when they walk in? Is there something obvious to do? Someone whose job it is to spot the person who looks a little lost?

It sounds simple. It is not common. Most events treat arrival as a logistics problem, not an experience problem.

## Belonging is designed, not felt

One of the most useful things I read while planning events came from a community design researcher who said: belonging is not something people feel spontaneously. It is something that is created through specific conditions. Shared context, visible inclusion, low-stakes ways to participate.

For the Science Society events, we started building in what I called entry points. Small, optional activities at the beginning of the event that gave people something to do and a reason to talk to someone they did not know. Not icebreakers in the cringe-inducing sense. Just structure. A way in.

The events where we did this well had different energy by the end. People stayed longer. The photos looked different.

## Scale is not the goal

When I started building Event Tree, a platform for discovering campus events at Dalhousie, I thought the problem was information. Students did not know what was happening on campus. If they knew, they would go.

That was partly true. But information is not the constraint for most people. Confidence is. Going to an event where you do not know anyone requires a certain kind of confidence that not everyone has, especially early in university.

What I came to understand is that community is built in small events, not large ones. The large event creates visibility. The small event creates connection. A platform that only surfaces big events is solving for the wrong thing.

## What I have stopped trying to do

I have stopped trying to design events that everyone will enjoy. The attempt to appeal to everyone produces events that no one cares deeply about.

Better to design for a specific person, with specific taste, and let the edges be sharp. The events I remember most vividly from my own experience as a student were not the biggest ones. They were the ones where someone had clearly thought about exactly who would be there and what they would want.

That specificity is what community feels like from the inside.
